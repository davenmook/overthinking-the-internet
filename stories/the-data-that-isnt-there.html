The data that isn't there.

In July 2024, Hurricane Beryl knocked out power across the Houston area. I live here. I want to know: did the internet notice, and can public data show it?

Dave Noel · working in public on GitHub

The question

Most data stories start with what's in the dataset. This one starts with what's missing. When the power goes out, people stop running speed tests, phones stop streaming, and home routers go dark. The absence of data becomes a signal of its own.

The question I'm chasing: using only public data, can you see where and when an outage hit, and how quickly connectivity came back?

The plan

  • Internet outage signals from IODA, Georgia Tech's Internet Outage Detection and Analysis project, which tracks regional connectivity in near real time.
  • Cell site outage reports from the FCC's Disaster Information Reporting System, which publishes the share of cell sites out of service by county during major storms.
  • Storm records from NOAA's Storm Events database, for the when and where.
  • Speed test counts from Ookla's open data, as a second check. It comes in quarterly tiles, so a storm that lasted days may get averaged away. Finding out is part of the fun.

How I'll keep myself honest

July is also peak summer travel in Houston, so fewer tests could just mean fewer people in town. I'll compare the same quarters in a year without a major storm before claiming anything. If the signal isn't there, that's the story, and I'll write it up that way.

First look: the internet noticed

IODA pings blocks of internet addresses across Texas every ten minutes and counts how many answer. On a normal July day, about 76,000 of them do. Then Beryl came ashore around 4 a.m. on July 8.

By midmorning, about one in ten of those blocks had gone quiet, and it stayed that way for a day and a half. Getting back to 99% took a full week. The same days in 2023 barely moved.

One more thing jumped out. The routing data (BGP, the internet's address book) hardly budged. The networks were still telling the internet they existed, even when nobody behind them could get online. That's the data that isn't there, in one chart.

Next up: the FCC's cell site outage reports and the speed test counts, to see whether they tell the same story.
